Infrared Sauna Before or After a Massage: Which Order Actually Works?

People add the sauna to their booking and then ask us, at the front desk, which order they are supposed to do it in. Here is the honest answer, and the reasoning behind it.

Thirty minutes of infrared heat is the most-added extra on our booking page, and it is also the one people are least sure how to use. Half of you want it first. Half of you assume it goes at the end, like a reward. Both can be right, but not for the same reason, and picking the wrong one for your goal wastes a perfectly good half hour.

So here is how we think about it, what the heat is actually doing, and the one popular sauna claim we are not going to repeat just because everyone else does.

The short answer

Sauna first if you want deeper pressure to feel good. Sauna after if you want the calm to last longer.

That is genuinely most of it. If you booked deep tissue because your shoulders have been bracing since March, warm up first. If you booked a Swedish or a head spa because you want to leave feeling like a different person, save the heat for the end. If you have no strong feeling either way, put it first. It is the order that changes the massage itself, and the massage is the part you are really paying for.

What the heat is actually doing

An infrared sauna warms you directly rather than heating the air around you, which is why it feels gentler than a traditional sauna at the same stated temperature. You sweat, but the room never gets that steam-room heaviness that makes some people bail at ten minutes.

That difference is worth understanding, because it is the reason the sauna pairs well with a massage at all. In a traditional hot room, the air does the work, so the air has to be punishing. You are essentially waiting for the heat to soak inward from your skin. Infrared skips that step and warms you at a lower ambient temperature, which is why most people can sit comfortably for a full half hour and hold a conversation while they do it. A thirty-minute traditional sauna is an endurance event for a lot of people. A thirty-minute infrared session is just quiet.

The part that matters for massage is simple thermodynamics meeting soft tissue. Warm tissue is more pliable than cold tissue. Heat widens blood vessels near the surface, blood moves more freely, and muscle that has been guarding tends to let go a little before anyone has touched it. Your therapist then starts from a better place. Instead of spending the first fifteen minutes convincing your traps that nothing bad is happening, they can get to the actual work.

That is a modest, physical effect, and we would rather describe it accurately than oversell it. Thirty minutes of warmth does not fix a chronic problem. It makes one hour on the table meaningfully more productive.

The case for going in first

Book the sauna before your session if you are coming in for pressure. Deep tissue is the clearest case: it is slow, specific work into layers that do not open easily, and it is considerably more comfortable when the tissue is already warm. Clients who warm up first tend to tolerate more pressure without the flinch-and-apologize cycle, which means the session goes deeper without ever hurting more.

It also helps if you are arriving straight from a desk, a cold car in February, or a flight. You are not just cold, you are compressed. Half an hour of quiet heat resets your posture and your breathing before the massage starts, and you stop mentally answering emails somewhere around minute twelve.

Practical note: shower or at least rinse and cool down for a few minutes between the sauna and the table. Going straight from sweating to face-down under a sheet is unpleasant for everyone involved. Build five minutes into your plan for it and tell us when you book so the timing lines up rather than eating into your hour.

One more thing worth knowing: warm tissue and numb tissue are not the same. The sauna should leave you loose, not woozy. If you come out lightheaded, that is a sign the session ran long or you arrived dehydrated, and it will make the massage worse rather than better. Thirty minutes, water beforehand, and you will be fine.

The case for saving it for the end

Book it after if the goal is the feeling, not the pressure. A massage leaves most people in a slow, floaty, unbothered state, and it usually fades on the drive home somewhere around 700 East. Thirty minutes of heat afterward extends that stretch. You are not adding a second treatment so much as refusing to let the first one end.

This is the better order for anything already gentle. A Swedish session, a Japanese head spa, or foot reflexology does not need warmed-up tissue to work, so the heat is not buying you anything on the front end. Put it at the back end and it buys you an extra half hour of quiet instead. It is also, frankly, the more luxurious way to structure an afternoon, and some visits are not about problem-solving at all.

Which one you should pick

Choose sauna first if you booked deep tissue or sports work, if you carry stiffness rather than stress, if you are coming in cold, or if you have ever asked a therapist to go lighter halfway through and then regretted it.

Choose sauna after if you booked a relaxation-led service, if you have somewhere quiet to be afterward, if heat makes you sleepy rather than loose, or if you simply want the visit to end on the softest possible note.

People do ask about doing both, and you can, though we rarely think it earns the extra time. Two half-hour sessions bracketing a massage makes for a long visit and a lot of heat, and by the end most people are less relaxed than they would have been with one. If you want the full afternoon, we would rather you put the sauna in one spot and spend the other thirty minutes on a longer massage. Ninety minutes on the table beats sixty minutes plus a second sweat, almost every time.

And skip the heat entirely if you are pregnant, are managing blood pressure or a heart condition, or know that saunas make you lightheaded. Talk to your doctor first, and book the massage on its own. It is a complete session without the add-on, and nobody at the front desk will think twice about it. If you want the pregnancy-appropriate version of a spa afternoon, our pricing page lists the prenatal options.

The sweat-and-toxins claim, and why we skip it

You will read, on a lot of spa websites, that a sauna after a massage helps sweat out the toxins your therapist just released. We are not going to tell you that, because it is not how the body works. Sweat is temperature control. It is mostly water and salt. The organs that handle clearance are your liver and kidneys, and they do not need a sauna appointment to get on with it.

What you do get is real enough to stand on its own: warmth, better circulation to the tissue, muscles that soften before anyone touches them, thirty minutes with no phone, and a nervous system that stops bracing. That is worth thirty dollars. It does not need a myth attached.

Do drink water afterward, though. You sweated, and that part is genuinely just fluid.
